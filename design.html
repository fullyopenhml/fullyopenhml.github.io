Design

One robot, two parents. The joint layout and actuator strategy come from Duke Humanoid V2; the manufacturing strategy comes from Berkeley Humanoid Lite. This page records every decision so that the next person can disagree with it precisely.

Goals

  1. Cost. Under $6,000 in parts for a robot that can walk and carry a gripper, without giving up integrated quasi-direct-drive actuators. Actuators are the floor: 23 RobStride units are about 80 % of the bill, so everything around them has to be nearly free.
  2. No machining. Every structural part prints on a desktop FDM printer with a 250 mm bed. The only metal is off-the-shelf: 2020 extrusion cut to length, thin-section bearings, screws and inserts.
  3. Few distinct parts. Four actuator models, three bearing sizes, one connector family. Every joint of a given actuator model uses the same printed adapter, cup and support bearing, so there are three joint kits repeated 23 times rather than 23 joints.
  4. Sourceable anywhere. Everything but the actuators comes from Amazon, AliExpress or any filament vendor, with the manufacturer part named so substitutes are obvious.
  5. Buildable by one person. Bench subassemblies that one person can lift, and a hoist only for the final steps.
  6. Honest numbers. Every figure on this site is either computed from the model in tools/build_data.py, copied from a named source, or marked as an estimate.

What is kept from each parent

From Duke Humanoid V2

  • Leg topology. Hip pitch → roll → yaw, knee, ankle pitch → roll. The hip-yaw actuator sits directly on top of the knee actuator, so the "thigh" is a short block and the long link is the shank. This keeps the hip narrow and the leg mass high.
  • RobStride actuators with the same model-to-joint logic: RS03 wherever gravity torque is large, RS06 for roll axes, RS02 for arms, RS05 for the wrist.
  • Bearing-supported joints. Duke's retainer–bearing–coupler stack on every output: a thin-section bearing between the printed mount ring and the coupler hub, so the printed coupler carries torque and the bearing carries the bending moment.
  • One-shell harness. XT30(2+2) carries 48 V and CAN in one connector; every limb is a daisy chain with a terminator at the end.
  • The parallel gripper (rack and pinion, one bus servo), as an optional kit.
  • Joint limits, the ID map pattern and the bring-up sequence.

From Berkeley Humanoid Lite

  • All structure printed. Plates, cups and adapters in carbon-filled PETG; covers in PLA; soles and pads in TPU.
  • 2020 aluminium extrusion torso with printed end plates: stiff, cheap, and cut with a hacksaw.
  • A $160 mini PC is enough to run a reinforcement-learning policy at 50 Hz and the motor loop at 500 Hz.
  • Blind by default. Walking uses the IMU and joint encoders. Cameras are an add-on kit, not a prerequisite.
  • Filament and fasteners listed in the BOM as real line items.
  • Two-configuration thinking: a core robot everyone builds the same way, plus kits.

Kinematics

Frame: X forward, Y left, Z up, ground at z = 0. The pelvis is the base link. Right-side joints are mirrored with the X and Z axes flipped so that a positive angle is the same motion (abduction, outward yaw) on both sides. The arm is straight: shoulder yaw, elbow and wrist share one line 232 mm from the centre, carried by a long shoulder-roll bracket as on Duke V2, so that yawing the shoulder spins the forearm about its own axis and the hand clears the hip actuators at every wrist angle.

Self-collision audit

tools/check_collisions.py tests every pair of parts: exactly at the zero pose, by surface sampling at the preset poses, and joint by joint through each range. The zero pose, the presets and every single-joint sweep are clear at the limits below; the limits marked geometry are where the printed parts would touch, and the sweep put them there.

One coupled limit. With the arm hanging straight, the leg can abduct 25° before the hip-yaw actuator meets the gripper. Swing the arm 20° forward, or flex the elbow 60°, and the full 105° is clear. That is a constraint for the policy's self-collision term, not a mechanical stop, and it is the same on Duke V2.

Open questions and risks

  • Printed couplers at 60 N·m. The RS03 output boss drives the printed coupler through six M4 screws and three Ø4 dowels on a 30.36 mm circle: 60 N·m is 4 kN on the dowels. That is at the edge of PETG-CF bearing strength; the v0.3 joint-kit test measures it. Fallbacks: PA-CF couplers, longer dowels, or a 3 mm aluminium coupler as the only metal part.
  • Heat. RobStride warns at 75 °C. PETG softens around 80 °C. The cups leave the finned housing exposed; if a joint runs hot, its cup goes to PA-CF.
  • Width. 562 mm across the shoulder-roll cups, wider than Duke V2, because the straight arm needs the roll axis 232 mm out and the hip-pitch actuators sit fully inside the pelvis. A canted hip-pitch axis as on Duke V2 would let the arms come in 20–30 mm at the cost of a harder-to-print bracket.
  • No mechanical end stops. Limits are software only, as on Duke V2.
  • Ankle stack height. The ankle-roll actuator sits behind the ankle-pitch actuator, which puts the heel actuator 76 mm behind the shank line. Reaction forces at heel strike go through the ankle bracket; it is the thickest printed part for that reason.
  • Nothing is measured. Masses, run time, print times and torque margins are calculated. The roadmap replaces each with a measurement.
